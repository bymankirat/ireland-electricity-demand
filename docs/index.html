Irish Electricity Demand: Growth, Wind and Day-Ahead Forecasting

Mankirat Singh · Portfolio · GitHub · LinkedIn

Republic of Ireland, hourly, January 2019 to 11 October 2026. Data: EirGrid Smart Grid Dashboard (system demand, wind generation, CO2 intensity) and Met Éireann (Dublin Airport temperature). Independent analysis, not an EirGrid or ESB publication.

Key findings

  • Electricity demand in the Republic of Ireland grew 19% from 2019 to 2025 (29.1 to 34.6 TWh).
  • Demand peaks on winter weekday evenings: on an average winter weekday it reaches 5,210 MW around 18:00. The highest hour on record here was 5,965 MW on 08 January 2025.
  • Wind met 33% of demand over the last 12 months, and at least half of demand in 25% of hours.
  • Wind and carbon move together: each extra 10 points of wind share goes with about 21 g/kWh less CO2 (median 267 g/kWh in the least windy tenth of hours against 118 g/kWh in the windiest tenth).
  • A day-ahead forecast issued at midnight is accurate to 2.74% per hour on unseen data, a 34% improvement on the standard benchmark (4.13%). The daily peak is forecast within 119 MW on average.

2. When demand peaks

The system has to be built and operated for the peak, not the average. Winter weekday evenings, when lighting, heating and cooking coincide, are the hardest hours.

3. Wind and carbon

Over October 2024 to October 2026, the share of demand met by wind explains most of the hour-to-hour variation in carbon intensity (correlation -0.74).

4. Forecasting tomorrow's demand

Each night at midnight, the model forecasts every hour of the coming day, using only information available then: recent demand, the calendar, Irish public holidays. It was trained on data up to 2023 (leaving out the first COVID-19 lockdown), chosen on 2024, and then tested once on January 2025 onwards, a period never used to build or choose it.

Honest uncertainty. 80% prediction intervals built once from the 2024 errors contained the actual demand 70% of the time on the test period: too often wrong, because demand kept growing and the model was forecasting further from its training data, so its errors grew. Recalibrating the intervals every day from the previous 90 days of known errors (adaptive conformal prediction) gives 79% coverage (target 80%), with an average width of 330 MW against 266 MW. 5. What this means for an electricity company

  • Planning: steady demand growth and winter evening peaks drive the need for network capacity and flexible supply such as batteries and demand response.
  • Operations: a transparent day-ahead forecast with honest uncertainty bands supports scheduling and trading; its errors are largest around holidays and unusual weather, where human judgement should be added.
  • Decarbonisation: carbon intensity falls sharply as wind rises, so shifting flexible demand (for example EV charging) into windy hours is one of the cheapest ways to cut emissions.

Limitations and methods

  • Republic of Ireland only. System demand as published by EirGrid; it does not separate large users such as data centres.
  • Temperature is observed, not forecast (see section 4). One weather station (Dublin Airport) represents the country.
  • EirGrid publishes its own demand forecasts; comparing against them is a natural next step.
  • Methods: Python (pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, matplotlib). Ridge regression on hour × day-type, lagged demand and weather; histogram gradient boosting; permutation importance; fixed and adaptive (rolling 90-day) conformal prediction intervals. Irish public holidays computed from the official rules, including weekend substitutions and St Brigid's Day from 2023. Code: GitHub.
Data: EirGrid Smart Grid Dashboard; Met Éireann (CC BY 4.0). Analysis and any errors are the author's own.
